Efficient hardware designs of the new Versatile Video Coding (VVC) tools for ASIC platforms

For three years I worked on getting three of the new VVC tools to run in real time on a chip, for a company’s 4K encoder and decoder. This page explains the work in ten short parts, with more detail under each “Go deeper”.

The starting point

Every few years a new video standard halves the bits needed for the same picture quality. VVC did that in 2020. The cost is computation: the VVC encoder is about ten times heavier than HEVC’s and the decoder about twice. In a product, that computation has to be done in hardware.

Go deeper
  • Standards: H.261 (1990) → MPEG-2 (1993) → H.264/AVC (2003) → HEVC/H.265 (2013) → VVC/H.266 (July 2020), each developed jointly by ITU-T VCEG and ISO/IEC MPEG.
  • VVC: about 40 % bit-rate reduction in objective terms (thesis abstract) and about 50 % in subjective tests (JVET), at the same quality.
  • Complexity estimated at about 10× HEVC for the encoder and about 2× for the decoder (thesis §2.2).
  • Video was forecast at 82 % of internet traffic by 2023 (Cisco, cited in the thesis).

Sources: thesis §2.1–2.2 and §3.3; Bross et al., “Overview of the VVC standard”, IEEE TCSVT 2021.

What a codec does

A codec predicts each block of the picture from what it has already coded, keeps only the difference, and applies a transform that concentrates that difference into a few coefficients. The small coefficients are then rounded away. The decoder reverses these steps, and the encoder contains a full decoder so that both sides reconstruct the same picture.

Go deeper
  • Every modern codec is a hybrid coder: intra prediction (from neighbours in the picture) or inter prediction (from previous pictures, with motion), then transform, quantisation and entropy coding of the residual.
  • The encoder reconstructs exactly what the decoder will see, because future predictions must use the same pixels on both sides. In-loop filters clean that reconstruction before it becomes a reference.
  • Every encoder decision minimises a rate–distortion cost J = D + λ·R. Codecs are compared by Bjøntegaard delta rate under JVET common test conditions.
  • The figure runs a real 2-D DCT on its residual and quantises with a step of 16.

Sources: thesis §3.2–3.4.

The three tools

VVC added two transforms, MTS and LFNST, and a new in-loop filter, ALF. Each one saves a few percent of bit rate, which is significant in this field. They are small additions in the standard but costly in hardware. My thesis covers these three tools, for the decoder and for the encoder.

Go deeper
  • MTS: the encoder picks DCT-2, DST-7 or DCT-8 per direction (5 combinations) for luma blocks; DCT-2 up to 64×64. Switching it off costs +1.3 % bit rate in intra coding (VTM 8).
  • LFNST: a second, non-separable transform on the low-frequency corner of DCT-2 blocks; 4 sets × 2 kernels. Off: +1.0 % (luma), +2 % (chroma) in intra coding.
  • ALF: a 7×7 luma diamond, a 5×5 chroma diamond and a cross-component filter. Off: +2.2 % (luma) and +12.8 % (chroma U) in intra coding; ALF is about a quarter of the software decoder’s time.
  • Hardware cost comes from more multiplications, more kernels to store (3 MTS, 8 LFNST, 64 fixed ALF filters) and, for ALF, the pixel neighbourhoods kept in memory.

Sources: thesis §3.4, Tables 6.7 and 9.7, Fig. 9.12.

4 / 10

Why a chip and not software

Live video has a deadline for every frame. The company I worked with makes fanless encoders for vehicles and portable use, so software was not an option: a processor spends most of its energy moving instructions and data rather than computing. A chip designed for one task avoids that. The drawback is that a chip cannot be changed after manufacture, so the design has to be correct before it is made.

CPU · GPU · FPGA · ASIC, level by level →
Go deeper
  • The thesis cites a study by Xilinx: dedicated hardware (FPGA or ASIC) reaches higher quality at lower power than software for real-time video; and the rule of thumb of about 20× the performance of a CPU at about 20× lower power for an ASIC.
  • Hameed et al. measured a general-purpose processor at about 500× the energy of an ASIC for H.264 encoding; SIMD and VLIW extensions recovered about 10×; the remaining 50× needed custom datapaths.
  • Real-time encoders are judged on quality, latency, SWaP (size, weight, power) and feature set.
  • The partner’s previous (HEVC) generation ran on FPGAs; VVC moved to an ASIC once the standard was final (July 2020).

Sources: thesis §3.5; Hameed et al., “Understanding sources of inefficiency in general-purpose chips”, ISCA 2010.

The throughput requirement

4K at 60 frames per second on a 450 MHz chip means the transform must process four samples every clock cycle, for every block size. Every block must also take the same number of cycles, so that the following blocks never wait. Within that requirement, the goal was to make each block as small as possible in area, memory and power.

Go deeper
  • Decoder: 4K120 over two cores → 4K60 per core at 450 MHz. 3840×2160×60 luma samples ×1.5 (4:2:0 chroma) ≈ 747 M samples/s ≈ 1.7 per cycle on average; the module is sized for the worst case (small blocks, no zeroing): 4 samples/cycle for the 1-D transform, 2 for the folded 2-D transform, 2 pixels/cycle for ALF.
  • Cycle budget per transform block at 4K60: 64×64 → 1,238 cycles; 32×32 → 310; 16×16 → 78; 8×8 → 20; 4×4 → 5 (thesis Table 6.1).
  • Encoder: 4K60 over four cores → 1080p60 per core, with 9,000 cycles per 64×64 coding tree unit for all its transform candidates and loop filters.
  • Cost is reported after synthesis with Synopsys Design Compiler on TSMC 28 nm: cell area or gates, kilobits of RAM/ROM, milliwatts at 0.8 V and 85 °C.

Sources: thesis §5.1, §6.1, §7.1, §9.1, §10.1.

6 / 10

The transform

The usual way to build a transform in hardware is to hard-wire its constants, and for HEVC that was the cheapest option. VVC has many more transform types and sizes, so I built both versions. The result was the opposite: 64 shared multipliers reading their coefficients from a small ROM took 63 % less silicon. The LFNST was added in the same way. We did not find an earlier hardware implementation of LFNST in the literature.

Go deeper
  • My first design (ICASSP 2020): 1-D inverse MTS at 2 samples/cycle. Constant multipliers (shift-and-add, one module per size and type): 266,315 cell area. Regular multipliers (32 shared, 1,872 coefficients in a 17.4 Kbit ROM): 96,849. The multi-standard engine also covers AVC and HEVC.
  • The second (IEEE TCE 2021): 4 samples/cycle. MTS scales to 64 multipliers (+55 % area for 2× throughput, 138.9 Kgates); inverse LFNST added with its own 64 multipliers and a 64 Kbit ROM of the 8 kernels (109.2 Kgates). Unified: 128 multipliers, fixed latency, bypass delay line for blocks without LFNST.
  • Techniques used: butterfly decomposition of DCT-2 (sizes nest), DCT-8 = DST-7 with reversed inputs and flipped signs, zeroing of high frequencies for 64-point DCT-2 and 32-point DST-7/DCT-8.
  • Cost in perspective: at equal throughput the VVC transform (MTS + LFNST, 163,672 gates) is about 4× the HEVC transform (41,500 gates); MTS alone about 2×.
  • Encoder side: three transform chains of 32 samples/cycle; the forward DCT-2 shares its core with the inverse DCT-2 (symmetric odd kernels), LFNST kept separate.

Sources: thesis Ch. 5–7; Tables 5.4, 6.8–6.11, 7.7.

7 / 10

ALF, the adaptive loop filter

ALF is a 7×7 diamond-shaped filter whose coefficients the encoder computes and transmits. Each 4×4 block is classified into one of 25 classes, and the kernel is rotated or flipped accordingly. Because the filter is symmetric, one engine with 26 multipliers handles luma, chroma and the cross-component filter.

Go deeper
  • ALF is a Wiener filter: coefficients minimise the squared error between reconstruction and original; up to 25 luma sets and 8 chroma sets per adaptation parameter set, plus 64 fixed luma filters in the standard.
  • Point symmetry halves the multiplications: 12 unique luma coefficients, 6 chroma, 7 for the 3×4 cross-component filter.
  • Classification: sub-sampled Laplacian gradients in four directions give a directionality D (0–4) and an activity A (0–4); class = 5·D + A. The gradient ratios also choose rotation, vertical flip or diagonal flip of the kernel.
  • Filter engine: three pipeline stages (pixel selection → clip and add symmetric pairs → 26 multipliers), 2 pixels/cycle, one 4×4 block every 8 cycles, fixed latency for CTU sizes 16 to 128.

Sources: thesis Ch. 8 and §9.5; Karczewicz et al., “VVC in-loop filters”, IEEE TCSVT 2021.

The memory problem

To filter one block, ALF needs the eight blocks around it, so blocks have to wait in memory. Feeding the filter in the order Cr, Cb, Y, Y keeps the luma needed by the chroma filter already in the pipeline, and only two lines of blocks have to be stored. The memory is reduced by three quarters. The three orders can be compared above: outlined blocks are the ones held in memory.

Go deeper
  • I compared three feeding orders for a 128×128 CTU (4:2:2, 10-bit): A, whole luma first, keeps the entire luma CTB for the cross-component filter: 193.28 Kbit. B, chroma first, parks the filtered chroma until luma arrives: 213.12 Kbit. C, interleaved Cr, Cb, Y, Y per CTU line: two lines and two columns of blocks per component, 50.56 Kbit (35.84 for 4:2:0).
  • Second problem: a 4×4 block at 2 pixels/cycle is an 8-cycle window, but 8 neighbour reads plus 1 write take 9 cycles. Consecutive blocks share 6 of their 9 neighbours, so the second block needs only 3 operations: 12 per pair in a 16-cycle window.
  • Two RAMs: one serving a 4×4 block per cycle (160-bit port), one holding a one-sample column for the cross-component filter (40-bit port). The memory module is 48 % of the ALF area.
  • The figure is a simplified model: the outlined blocks are the ones still held in memory, and the gauge rises to the value measured for each order.

Sources: thesis §9.3–9.4, Tables 9.3–9.6; IEEE TCE 2022.

In numbers
  • Inverse MTS + LFNST: 128 multipliers, 4 samples per cycle.
  • ALF decoder: 26 multipliers, 2 pixels per cycle, 50.6 Kbit of RAM, about 10 mW.
  • All of it synthesised at 450 MHz on TSMC 28 nm, verified against the reference software.
  • Papers: ICASSP 2020, IEEE TCE 2021, IEEE TCE 2022, PCS 2022.
9 / 10

Results

Every block runs at 450 MHz on a 28 nm process with a fixed latency, and matches the reference software bit for bit: a hundred thousand random test vectors for the transforms, complete encoded sequences for the filter. The blocks were integrated in the company’s real-time encoder and decoder, and four papers came out of the work. One limitation: the encoder-side ALF handles only luma and 64×64 blocks, because the full version did not fit the cycle budget.

All the numbers, in the long version →
Go deeper
  • Decoder: inverse MTS 64 RM, 138.9 Kgates; inverse LFNST 64 RM, 109.2 Kgates, 64 Kbit ROM; ALF 26 RM, 66,997 µm², 50.56 Kbit RAM, 9.6 mW at 0.8 V (6.7 mW at 0.65 V for +12 % area).
  • Encoder: transform loop as three chains of 32 samples/cycle with a shared DCT/iDCT core; ALF luma filter 24 RM, 41,984 µm², 13.6 mW; ALF decision (4 filters + SSD) 48 RM, 58,628 µm², 101.6 Kbit, 17.9 mW. Encoder ALF limited to luma, CTU 64 and 4 of 16 filter sets.
  • Verification: 100,000 pseudo-random vectors per transform module against VTM procedures; ALF against trace files from the instrumented software encoder, sequences from 64×64 to 1080p in 8, 10 and 12 bits.
  • Possible next steps named in the thesis: one 64-multiplier core shared by MTS and LFNST; a hybrid transform with hard-wired constants for DCT-2 only; a learned shortcut for the encoder’s transform search; a chroma filter in the encoder ALF.

Sources: thesis §5.4, §6.5, §9.6, §10.4, Ch. 11; Appendix A.
